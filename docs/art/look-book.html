FROM THE JOURNAL

Why it fits

The journal is the heart of your story, so the whole interface becomes the journal. Unknown words can look like real handwriting in another script. It's text-heavy, which suits a decision game.

Effort for us

Text and layout are cheap. The ink drawings need one consistent hand, from a hired illustrator or a well-chosen asset pack.

Look up

  • Pentiment: medieval manuscript art, and text that's lettered as it appears
  • Heaven's Vault: translating an ancient script word by word
  • 80 Days: an elegant, text-first interface

Why it fits

Your people start out primal, and the art can grow with them: cave paintings first, then pottery patterns, then carved stone. The art style becomes a record of your civilization's progress, just as the journal's words do.

Effort for us

Simple painted shapes are forgiving, and rough edges look intentional. If the style changes with each era, though, every era needs its own art pass.

Look up

  • Lascaux and Chauvet cave paintings: the real source material
  • Ōkami: a god's world drawn in brush strokes

FROM THE JOURNAL

Why it fits

Your decision web becomes the sky. Each discovery lights a star, and branches you've closed off stay dark. You look down on your people from the Pantheon, which is exactly where the story puts you.

Effort for us

Lines, dots and glows can be drawn entirely in code, which makes this the cheapest style here. It doesn't cover the mortal world, though, so it pairs best with another style.

Look up

  • Hades: gods, Olympus, and beautiful divine interface design
  • Sky: Children of the Light: soft celestial glow
  • Path of Exile's passive tree: a huge decision web drawn like a star map

Why it fits

The sky's colors can tell the story on their own: warm when faith is strong, grey when Memory interferes, strange colors when Time skips a generation. Mood changes cost almost nothing because they're only color changes.

Effort for us

The most beginner-friendly option. It's flat shapes with no faces or detailed characters, and color changes can be done in code. It looks polished early on.

Look up

  • Alto's Odyssey: gradient skies and layered silhouettes
  • Kingdom Two Crowns: a side-view village with changing light (pixel art)
  • Badland: dark foreground silhouettes against colorful backgrounds

Why it fits

Prayer cards are the core of every turn, and this style puts them front and center. Recurring characters like Sana become recognizable faces you get attached to across generations.

Effort for us

Each portrait is simple, but every character needs one, and a game across many generations has a lot of characters. Reusing portraits for descendants (Sana's granddaughter wearing her headdress) helps.

Look up

  • Reigns: the direct inspiration for prayer cards
  • Sort the Court: charming kingdom decisions in a simple style
  • Cultist Simulator: cards laid on a table, with an occult mystery

A. Heaven and Earth

The village sits at the bottom of the screen. Scroll up into the sky, where your decision web hangs as constellations. Prayers rise from the village like smoke.

Fits because the story's geography becomes the interface: gods above, mortals below.

Left page: the world, drawn
Right page: prayer and choices
Flip back: recovered journal

B. The Open Book

The whole game takes place inside the journal. Each turn is a new spread, and turning back shows the pages you've recovered.

Fits because the journal is the center of the story. Cheap to build. The risk is that it can feel static.

C. The Altar

You look down at an altar where your people leave prayers and offerings. You pick them up and answer them. Faith piles up as tokens you can see.

Fits because it's tactile and ritualistic. It needs the most interface art of the three.

My recommendation

Heaven and Earth: three styles, one for each world

Your story has three places: the Pantheon, the mortal world, and the journal that connects them. Each one can get its own style, which helps players always know where they are.

The Pantheon: Celestial

Faith, your divine rank, the decision web as constellations, and every appearance by Time and Memory. Gold on night.

The mortal world: Silhouette & Sky

Your village and people. It's cheap to make, and the sky's colors show mood: warm faith, Memory's grey fog, Time's strange light.

The journal: Ink & Parchment

Her people's language in handwritten glyphs. It's the one physical object from the past, so it should look different from everything else. For language design ideas, look up Chants of Sennaar.

Why I'd choose this: the two styles that cover most of the screen are the cheapest to produce, so most of the budget can go to the journal and the store page artwork. It also uses Layout A, where the decision web lives in the sky above your people.
